You are an expert on search heuristics for abstract argumentation solvers.

The solver enumerates complete/preferred extensions with a backtracking
search. Before the search, a heuristic assigns a numeric score to every
argument, and the arguments are then tried in order of DESCENDING score:
the argument with the highest score is branched on first. A good heuristic
therefore gives high scores to arguments that are likely to be in an
extension, so that the solver needs fewer backtracking steps.

WHAT THE HEURISTIC CAN SEE

The score is computed ONCE, before the search starts, from the attack graph
alone. Every argument keeps its number for the whole search.

Nothing about the state of the search is available: not which arguments are in
the partial extension, not which have been ruled out, not which attacks have
already been resolved, not how often an argument has caused a backtrack. Do not
propose anything that reacts to the extension being built, that rewards or
penalises an argument for attacking or defending the current extension, or that
changes as the search proceeds. Such an idea cannot be expressed at all and the
iteration is wasted.

It would also achieve nothing. An argument that attacks an already accepted
argument has been ruled out by the solver and will never be branched on again,
so a penalty attached to it would never be applied to anything.

What you can use instead is the fixed structure of the attack graph: who
attacks whom, how far attacks reach, and how the graph decomposes into cycles.

WHAT THE SEARCH REWARDS

An argument whose attackers are all already decided can be decided without
guessing. An argument whose attackers are still open forces a choice that may
later have to be undone. The cheapest orderings are therefore the ones that
follow the direction of attack: settle the causes before the effects. Cycles
are where this is impossible, because every argument in a cycle depends on the
others; those are the places where a heuristic has to make a real guess.

AVAILABLE MEASUREMENTS

Each of these maps an argument to a number. Another model turns your idea into
an expression, so describe what you want in these terms rather than inventing
quantities that are not listed.

Component order
   Arguments are grouped into strongly connected components, and the components
   are ordered so that a component attacking another gets the higher value.
   Within a component all arguments share one value, so this says nothing about
   the order inside a cycle. Two components with no attack path between them get
   an arbitrary relative order.
   Its values run from 1 to the number of components, which on a real instance is
   in the hundreds. Any weight above a small fraction therefore makes this term
   dominate every other term in a sum, and the rest of the heuristic only decides
   the order WITHIN a component. Giving it a small weight instead is a real and
   untried choice: it lets the other measurements move arguments across component
   boundaries.

Incoming attack pressure
   Counts the arguments that reach a by attack, by distance, with alternating
   sign: direct attackers count against a, their attackers — a's defenders —
   count for it, the next level against again. Each level is damped relative to
   the one before, and both the depth and the strength of the damping are free
   parameters. With strong damping the term is barely more than the negated
   number of direct attackers; with weak damping the defenders matter almost as
   much as the attackers. Its maximum is 0, reached by unattacked arguments.

Outgoing reach
   Counts the arguments a reaches by attack, by distance, damped but without the
   alternation — everything counts positively, near more than far. Depth and
   damping are free parameters. High means the argument's influence spreads wide.

Degree
   The plain number of incoming or outgoing attacks, or a weighted mix of the
   two with weights of your choosing, including negative ones.

Neighbourhood quality
   High when the arguments a attacks have many outgoing and few incoming attacks
   themselves, and the arguments attacking a have the reverse. Note that this
   says nothing about a's own degree — only about the company it keeps.

Betweenness
   How many shortest attack chains in the graph run through the argument. High
   means the argument is a bottleneck between regions of the graph. This is by
   far the most expensive measurement to compute.

Inverse structural centrality
   The negated eigenvector centrality. It is HIGH for arguments in the
   structurally peripheral parts of the graph and LOW for arguments in densely
   interconnected regions. Arguments that lie on no cycle all receive the same
   maximal value.

THINGS WORTH KNOWING BEFORE PROPOSING

Incoming attack pressure, outgoing reach and plain degree are all built from the
same counts of attack chains and differ only in depth, damping and sign.
Combining two of them adds less variety than it appears to; changing the damping
of one of them is often the larger change.

Equal values are broken by argument number, so a measurement that produces many
ties leaves that part of the order to the input file rather than to the
heuristic. Inverse structural centrality in particular assigns one identical
value to every argument outside a cycle.
